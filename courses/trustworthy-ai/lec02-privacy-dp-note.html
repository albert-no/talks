Privacy & Differential Privacy

Welcome to Lecture 2 of Trustworthy AI. Today we ask a simple question: what does a trained model actually leak about the people in its data, and can we write down a mathematical definition of "private"?

Key takeaway: Privacy is a property we can define and prove, not just hope for.

Contents

We move in six steps: why models leak, how we measure that leakage, the definition of differential privacy, how we achieve it with noise, private machine learning, and the 2025-26 frontier.

Key takeaway: From the problem, to a definition, to practical training under a budget.

01 — The Privacy Problem

First section. The core idea: a trained model is a lossy copy of its training data, and lossy does not mean safe.

Key takeaway: A model carries traces of the people it was trained on.

Models Are Data-Hungry

Modern models train on everything within reach: scraped web pages, books, code, photos, sometimes even health and financial records. That data describes real people, not abstractions.

Key takeaway: Training data is people's data.

A Model Remembers

Training presses data into the weights. Most blends into general patterns, but some survives nearly verbatim, and rare or repeated examples stick the most.

Key takeaway: "It's just statistics" is not a privacy guarantee.

Definitions

Extractable memorization (Nasr et al. 2023): a training string $s$ is extractably memorized by a model $M$ if an adversary, without prior knowledge of the training set, can construct a prompt $p$ such that $M(p)$ emits $s$ verbatim. This is the operational notion behind every attack in this section: memorization is defined by what an attacker can pull out, not by what the weights "contain."

Background & further reading

  • Nasr et al., "Scalable Extraction of Training Data from (Production) Language Models" (2023) — defines extractable memorization and demonstrates it at scale, from open models to ChatGPT.

GPT-2 Knew Real People

Carlini and colleagues showed that a short prompt can make GPT-2 recite real names, home addresses, phone numbers, and emails, copied straight out of the training text.

Key takeaway: A short prompt can pull out a real person's contact details.

Background & further reading

Carlini et al. (USENIX Security 2021) formalized the training-data extraction attack: query the model, collect high-likelihood generations, and filter with membership signals. Against GPT-2 (trained on public web scrapes) they extracted hundreds of verbatim training sequences, including personally identifiable information — names, phone numbers, and email addresses of real people. The slide's figure is Figure 1 of this paper.

  • Carlini et al., "Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models," USENIX Security 2021 — the GPT-2 extraction study.

The Secret Sharer

The clean experiment: plant a fake secret, a "canary" like a made-up credit-card number, in the data. After enough training the model memorizes it, and the right prompt makes it spill.

Key takeaway: Memorization is measurable: plant a secret and watch it come back.

Definitions

Canary: a synthetic secret sequence $s[r]$ (a fixed format $s$ filled with a random value $r$, e.g. a fake credit-card number) inserted into the training data, chosen so that it cannot be learned from anything else in the corpus.

Exposure (Carlini et al. 2019, Def. 4): with randomness space $\mathcal R$ (all possible fill-in values $r$), $$\mathrm{exposure}_\theta(s[r]) \;=\; \log_2 |\mathcal R| \;-\; \log_2 \mathrm{rank}_\theta(s[r]),$$ where $\mathrm{rank}_\theta(s[r])$ is the rank of the inserted canary among all candidates, ordered by the model's log-perplexity. Exposure $\approx 0$ means the model ranks the true secret no better than chance; exposure near $\log_2|\mathcal R|$ means the secret is the model's top guess and can be extracted by search.

Background & further reading

  • Carlini, Liu, Erlingsson, Kos, Song, "The Secret Sharer: Evaluating and Testing Unintended Memorization in Neural Networks," USENIX Security 2019 — introduces the canary/exposure methodology; finds unintended memorization is persistent and not simply a symptom of overfitting.

Make ChatGPT Leak

In 2023, asking ChatGPT to "repeat the word 'poem' forever" eventually broke the loop and dumped chunks of raw training data, including real names and contact info.

Key takeaway: Even production chat models can be tricked into leaking training data.

Background & further reading

Nasr et al. (2023) showed that existing extraction techniques suffice against unaligned open models (Pythia, GPT-Neo, LLaMA, Falcon), but aligned ChatGPT resists them — so they introduced the divergence attack: asking the model to repeat a word forever eventually breaks it out of its chatbot-style distribution, after which it emits raw training data, including personal contact information. In aggregate the paper extracts gigabytes of training data across model families.

  • Nasr et al., "Scalable Extraction of Training Data from (Production) Language Models" (2023) — the "repeat the word poem forever" divergence attack on ChatGPT.

Image Models Copy Too

It is not just text. Stable Diffusion regenerates near-exact copies of real training photos, especially images that were duplicated in the dataset. The figure is from Carlini et al.'s 2023 extraction study: a real training photo of a real person, next to the model's near-identical generation.

Key takeaway: Generative image models can reproduce identifiable training photos.

Background & further reading

Carlini et al. (USENIX Security 2023) ran a generate-and-filter pipeline against diffusion models and extracted over a thousand near-copies of training examples from state-of-the-art models — from photographs of individual people to trademarked logos. By training hundreds of diffusion models they also isolated which modeling and data decisions drive memorization; duplicated training images are the most vulnerable.

  • Carlini et al., "Extracting Training Data from Diffusion Models," USENIX Security 2023 — source of the slide's real-vs-generated portrait pair.

Copilot Leaks Secrets

Code models train on public repos, secrets and all. Live API keys, passwords, and tokens left in old commits can be suggested right back by GitHub Copilot — a 2024 study extracted thousands of hard-coded credentials from its suggestions, some of them real, live secrets.

Key takeaway: A secret in the data becomes a secret in the model.

Background & further reading

Huang, Li, Wu, Zhang, and Lyu built the Hard-coded Credential Revealer (HCR): prompts constructed from GitHub files that originally contained credentials, used to test whether commercial code-completion tools regurgitate them. The study (published in Proc. ACM Softw. Eng. / FSE 2024) shows neural code-completion tools, including GitHub Copilot, emit memorized hard-coded credentials — the concrete leak class behind this slide.

  • Huang et al., "Your Code Secret Belongs to Me: Neural Code Completion Tools Can Memorize Hard-Coded Credentials," FSE 2024 — the HCR study.

The Anonymization Myth

Deleting names does not anonymize data. Quasi-identifiers like ZIP, age, sex, and dates remain, and combining them with a public list re-identifies people.

Key takeaway: Anonymity is about linkage, not about one missing column.

Definitions

Quasi-identifier: a set of attributes (e.g. ZIP code, birth date, sex) none of which identifies a person alone, but whose combination is unique or near-unique in the population and can be joined against an external identified dataset (Sweeney 2000).

Linkage attack: re-identification by joining a "de-identified" release with a public identified dataset on the quasi-identifier columns. Sweeney's classic demonstration joined an anonymized state employee health-insurance release with the purchased Cambridge, MA voter roll on {ZIP, birth date, sex}.

$k$-anonymity (Sweeney, IJUFKS 2002): a release satisfies $k$-anonymity if every record is indistinguishable from at least $k-1$ others on the quasi-identifier attributes. It is a syntactic property of one table — it makes no guarantee against attackers with richer side information, which is exactly the gap DP later closes.

Background & further reading

  • Sweeney, "Simple Demographics Often Identify People Uniquely," Data Privacy WP3, 2000 — quasi-identifiers and the linkage methodology.
  • Sweeney, "k-Anonymity: A Model for Protecting Privacy," IJUFKS 10(5):557–570, 2002 — the formal $k$-anonymity model.

87% From Three Fields

Latanya Sweeney's 2000 study estimated that ZIP code, birth date, and sex together uniquely identified 87% of Americans on 1990 census data; a later re-estimate on 2000 census data put it at 63% — still a majority. Cross "anonymous" medical data against a public voter roll and you get a name.

Key takeaway: Three innocuous fields are enough to single out most people.

Background & further reading

The exact numbers: Sweeney (2000), using 1990 US Census summary data, estimated that 87% of the US population (216 of 248 million) was uniquely identified by {5-digit ZIP, full birth date, sex}. Golle (WPES 2006) re-ran the estimate on Census 2000 data and obtained 63% — lower, but still a solid majority from three "harmless" fields.

  • Sweeney, "Simple Demographics Often Identify People Uniquely," 2000 — the 87% estimate.
  • Golle, "Revisiting the Uniqueness of Simple Demographics in the US Population," WPES 2006 — the 63% re-estimate on Census 2000.

Netflix and AOL: Linkage Wins

Two famous "anonymized" releases, both broken. Netflix movie ratings were linked to named users via public IMDb reviews; AOL's 2006 search logs used random IDs, but the queries themselves named the user and a journalist traced one to a real person.

Key takeaway: The content is the identifier; sparse "anonymous" data links to public records.

Background & further reading

Netflix Prize (2006–2008). Netflix published anonymized movie ratings of about 500,000 subscribers as a recommender-system contest dataset. Narayanan and Shmatikov showed that high-dimensional sparse data defeats anonymization: an adversary who knows only a few of a subscriber's ratings (with imprecise dates) can identify the subscriber's full record, and they demonstrated linkage against public IMDb reviews. Their attack is robust to perturbation of the data.

AOL search logs (2006). AOL released three months of search queries — about 20 million queries — with user names replaced by random numeric IDs. Days later, New York Times reporters Michael Barbaro and Tom Zeller Jr. traced user No. 4417749 through her own queries to Thelma Arnold, a 62-year-old widow in Lilburn, Georgia. The identifier was removed; the content itself identified her.

  • Narayanan & Shmatikov, "How To Break Anonymity of the Netflix Prize Dataset" (2006; published as "Robust De-anonymization of Large Sparse Datasets," IEEE S&P 2008) — the Netflix de-anonymization.
  • Barbaro & Zeller, "A Face Is Exposed for AOL Searcher No. 4417749," New York Times, Aug 9, 2006 — the AOL re-identification story.

02 — How Leakage Is Measured

Second section. We turn the hunch that "models leak" into a number, using concrete attacks.

Key takeaway: Attacks let us quantify leakage instead of just worrying about it.

The Attacker's Toolkit

Four standard probes: membership inference (was this in training?), model inversion (rebuild a typical input), extraction (emit memorized text), and gradient leakage (invert a shared update).

Key takeaway: Four named attacks cover most privacy threats to a model.

Definitions

  • Membership inference: given a record $x$ and (black-box) access to $\theta$, decide whether $x$ was in the training set (Shokri et al. 2017).
  • Model inversion: given $\theta$ and a target label (e.g. a person's name in a face classifier), reconstruct a representative input for that label by exploiting the model's confidence outputs (Fredrikson et al. 2015).
  • Extraction: craft prompts that make a generative model emit memorized training sequences verbatim (Carlini et al. 2021).
  • Gradient leakage: given a gradient $g = \nabla_\theta \mathcal L(\theta; x, y)$ shared during distributed training, invert it to recover the private input $(x, y)$ (Zhu et al. 2019).

Background & further reading

  • Shokri, Stronati, Song, Shmatikov, "Membership Inference Attacks against Machine Learning Models" (IEEE S&P 2017)
  • Fredrikson, Jha, Ristenpart, "Model Inversion Attacks that Exploit Confidence Information and Basic Countermeasures," CCS 2015
  • Carlini et al., "Extracting Training Data from Large Language Models," USENIX Security 2021
  • Zhu, Liu, Han, "Deep Leakage from Gradients," NeurIPS 2019

Membership Inference

A membership inference attack asks one crisp question: was this specific record used to train the model? It is the unit test of privacy, the simplest leak to measure. And when the training cohort is defined by something sensitive, a diagnosis or a trial, that single yes-or-no bit is already the whole secret. The mechanics of the attack, from the 2008 genome-study case to likelihood-ratio attacks on neural networks, get a full lecture of their own.

Key takeaway: Membership inference is the baseline measurable leak.

Definitions

Membership-inference game (formulation: course notes). A challenger flips $b \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(1/2)$; if $b = 1$ it trains $\theta$ on a dataset containing the target $x$, if $b = 0$ on the same dataset without $x$. The adversary sees $\theta$ (or query access) and $x$, and outputs $\hat b$. Its advantage is $\mathrm{TPR} - \mathrm{FPR} = \Pr[\hat b = 1 \mid b = 1] - \Pr[\hat b = 1 \mid b = 0]$. Shokri et al. instantiate the attack by training "shadow models" on data like the target's, then training a classifier to distinguish member from non-member prediction vectors.

Theorem & proof

DP bounds every membership test (hypothesis-testing view; stated in the privacy course dp2 deck, citing Wasserman & Zhou, "A Statistical Framework for Differential Privacy," JASA 2010). If $M$ is $\varepsilon$-DP, then any test distinguishing neighbors $D$ (with Alice) from $D'$ (without) with false-alarm probability $q$ and miss probability $p$ satisfies $$1 \le p + e^{\varepsilon} q \qquad\text{and}\qquad 1 \le q + e^{\varepsilon} p.$$ Proof. Let $T$ be the test's rejection region (outputs on which it says "Alice is in"). By $\varepsilon$-DP, $1 - p = \Pr[M(D) \in T] \le e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[M(D') \in T] = e^{\varepsilon} q$, so $1 \le p + e^{\varepsilon} q$; swapping the roles of $D, D'$ gives the second inequality. $\blacksquare$ (proof: privacy course 01-dp, dp2 deck)

Consequence: with small $\varepsilon$, no attacker — regardless of side information — can be simultaneously sensitive and specific. This is why membership inference is exactly the attack DP is built to stop.

Background & further reading

  • Shokri et al., "Membership Inference Attacks against Machine Learning Models" (IEEE S&P 2017) — the shadow-model attack that started the field.

Model Inversion

Fredrikson and colleagues turned a model's confidence scores into a reconstructed face: given a name and access to the model, output a recognizable image of that person.

Key takeaway: Confidence outputs can be inverted into a recognizable input.

Definitions

Model inversion as optimization (formulation: course notes, following Fredrikson et al.): for a classifier $f_\theta$ with a target class $y$ (a person's name), reconstruct $$\hat x \;=\; \arg\max_{x} \; f_\theta(x)_y,$$ i.e. gradient ascent in input space on the confidence assigned to the target class, optionally with an image prior. The output is not a training image, but a class-representative reconstruction — for a face classifier trained on one person per class, that is a recognizable face.

Background & further reading

  • Fredrikson, Jha, Ristenpart, "Model Inversion Attacks that Exploit Confidence Information and Basic Countermeasures," CCS 2015 — recovers recognizable face images given only a name and model access; source of the slide's figure.

Can We Do Better?

Every attack so far targets a specific adversary. What we really want is a guarantee against any attacker, known or future, with any side knowledge. That is what differential privacy promises.

Key takeaway: We need protection against all attackers, not just today's.

03 — Differential Privacy

Third section. Differential privacy is a promise made to each individual, expressed as a property of the algorithm.

Key takeaway: DP shifts privacy from a hope to a provable guarantee.

A 2006 Idea

Differential privacy, introduced by Dwork and colleagues in 2006, is a mathematical definition of privacy. Not a patch or a heuristic, but a property an algorithm provably has or lacks.

Key takeaway: DP is a definition you can prove an algorithm satisfies.

Background & further reading

The attribution, precisely: pure $\varepsilon$-DP and the Laplace mechanism are from Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, and Smith, "Calibrating Noise to Sensitivity in Private Data Analysis" (TCC 2006). The $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ relaxation is from Dwork, Kenthapadi, McSherry, Mironov, and Naor, "Our Data, Ourselves: Privacy via Distributed Noise Generation" (EUROCRYPT 2006). The standard textbook treatment is Dwork & Roth (2014), free online.

  • Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, Smith, "Calibrating Noise to Sensitivity in Private Data Analysis," TCC 2006 — $\varepsilon$-DP and the Laplace mechanism.
  • Dwork, Kenthapadi, McSherry, Mironov, Naor, "Our Data, Ourselves," EUROCRYPT 2006 — the $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ relaxation.
  • Dwork & Roth, "The Algorithmic Foundations of Differential Privacy" (2014), free PDF — the reference text for everything in this lecture.

The Core Promise

The promise in one line: your presence in the data barely changes what comes out. Whatever the algorithm releases, it would have released almost the same thing without you.

Key takeaway: Being in or out of the data barely changes the output.

Two Worlds

Picture two datasets, one with Alice and one without. Run the same algorithm on both. DP demands that outputs A and B look alike.

Key takeaway: The world with you and the world without you should look the same.

Plausible Deniability

Make it concrete. The survey reports "41.3% say yes." Ask: how likely was that exact output if Alice was in the data, and how likely if she was not? DP says the two bars are within a factor $e^{\varepsilon}$ of each other, for every possible output. So an attacker who sees the output can update their odds that Alice participated by at most $e^{\varepsilon}$: at $\varepsilon = 1$, less than a factor of three. Alice can therefore say "I was never in that dataset," and nothing in the release seriously contradicts her. That sentence is the human meaning of the definition.

Key takeaway: Every output is nearly as likely without Alice as with her, so she can deny participating.

Definitions

Log-odds form of pure DP (source: lec02tech / privacy course): $M$ is $\varepsilon$-DP iff for all neighbors $D \sim D'$ and every output $y$, $$\left| \log \frac{\Pr[M(D) = y]}{\Pr[M(D') = y]} \right| \le \varepsilon.$$ Bayesian reading of deniability: whatever prior odds an observer holds on "Alice is in the data" vs "Alice is not," seeing the output multiplies those odds by at most $e^{\varepsilon}$, since posterior odds = prior odds × likelihood ratio and the likelihood ratio is bounded by $e^{\pm\varepsilon}$. With $\varepsilon$ small, no output can make the observer much more confident than they already were — that is plausible deniability, quantified.

Future-Proof by Design

DP makes no assumption about the attacker: any side information they have today, any database released tomorrow, the guarantee still holds. This is exactly what the de-anonymization stories lacked.

Key takeaway: DP holds against unknown and future attackers.

Randomness Is Required

A deterministic release cannot be DP: same input always gives the same output, so one changed record is detectable. DP algorithms are always randomized; randomness is the source of deniability.

Key takeaway: No randomness, no privacy.

Theorem & proof

Theorem. No deterministic, non-constant mechanism is $\varepsilon$-DP for any finite $\varepsilon$.

Proof. Let $f$ be deterministic and non-constant, so there exist datasets $A, B$ with $f(A) \ne f(B)$. Transform $A$ into $B$ one record at a time; along this chain of neighboring datasets the value of $f$ must change at some step, so there exist neighbors $D \sim D'$ with $f(D) \ne f(D')$. Take $S = \{f(D)\}$. Then $\Pr[f(D) \in S] = 1$ while $\Pr[f(D') \in S] = 0$, so the ratio $\Pr[f(D) \in S] / \Pr[f(D') \in S]$ is infinite and no finite $\varepsilon$ satisfies the DP inequality. $\blacksquare$ (derivation: course notes, generalizing the deterministic-mean counterexample in privacy course dp2)

Interpretation: an attacker who knows everyone's data except Alice's can compute the deterministic output for both hypotheses about Alice and read her record off the released value. Only randomness prevents this.

Formal Definition

Here is the definition. For neighboring datasets $D, D'$ and every outcome set $S$, $\Pr[M(D)\in S]\le e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[M(D')\in S]+\delta$. The map $M$ is randomized; small $\varepsilon,\delta$ mean the two worlds look alike.

Key takeaway: $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP bounds the output probability ratio between neighbors.

Definitions

$(\varepsilon,\delta)$-differential privacy (Dwork, Kenthapadi, McSherry, Mironov, Naor, EUROCRYPT 2006). A randomized mechanism $M : \mathcal D \to \mathcal Y$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP if $$\forall\, D \sim D' \;\; \forall\, \text{measurable } S \subseteq \mathcal Y: \quad \Pr[M(D) \in S] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\, \Pr[M(D') \in S] + \delta,$$ where the probability is over $M$'s internal randomness only, and the condition is required for every neighboring pair and every event — a worst-case guarantee, with no distributional assumption on the data.

Pure $\varepsilon$-DP (Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, Smith, TCC 2006) is the case $\delta = 0$. Since neighboring is symmetric, pure DP is equivalent to the two-sided bound $$e^{-\varepsilon} \;\le\; \frac{\Pr[M(D) \in S]}{\Pr[M(D') \in S]} \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}$$ for all neighbors and all $S$ with nonzero denominator (source: privacy course dp2).

Note the quantifier structure: DP is a property of the algorithm $M$, not of any particular dataset or output. That is what makes it provable.

Definitions

Convention used in this course (add/remove, "unbounded DP"): $D \sim D'$ iff $D'$ can be obtained from $D$ by adding or removing exactly one record, i.e. the multiset symmetric difference satisfies $|D \,\triangle\, D'| = 1$. This is the convention the deck and the tech supplement use ("take $D$, remove one row, get $D'$"); note $|D'| = |D| \pm 1$.

Alternative (replace, "bounded DP"): $D = (X_1,\dots,X_i,\dots,X_n)$ and $D' = (X_1,\dots,X_i',\dots,X_n)$ differ in one record's value, with $|D| = |D'| = n$. The privacy course (dp2) states this as its primary definition, with add/remove as the variant. The two are equivalent up to a factor of at most 2 in $\varepsilon$: a replacement is one removal followed by one addition, so an $\varepsilon$-DP mechanism under add/remove is $2\varepsilon$-DP under replace, by group privacy below.

Theorem & proof

Group privacy (Dwork & Roth 2014). If $M$ is $\varepsilon$-DP and $D, D''$ differ in at most $t$ records, then for every event $S$, $$\Pr[M(D) \in S] \;\le\; e^{t\varepsilon}\, \Pr[M(D'') \in S].$$ Proof. Build a telescoping chain $D = D_0 \sim D_1 \sim \cdots \sim D_t = D''$ in which consecutive datasets are neighbors (change the differing records one at a time). Applying the DP inequality at each hop, $$\Pr[M(D_0) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[M(D_1) \in S] \le e^{2\varepsilon} \Pr[M(D_2) \in S] \le \cdots \le e^{t\varepsilon} \Pr[M(D_t) \in S]. \;\blacksquare$$ (proof: privacy course 01-dp, dp3 deck)

Consequence: DP protects small groups at linearly degraded strength — a family of 4 under $\varepsilon = 0.5$ still has a factor-$e^{2} \approx 7.4$ guarantee, but for $t = 20$ the bound $e^{10}$ is vacuous. DP is an individual-level guarantee by design.

In Plain Words

In plain words: when one person joins or leaves, the probability of any output changes by at most a factor $e^{\varepsilon}$, plus a tiny slack $\delta$, for every possible output. Read $e^{\varepsilon}$ as "a multiplier near 1."

Key takeaway: One person can only nudge any outcome's odds by a small factor.

Definitions

The slack $\delta$. In $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP, $\delta$ is additive slack on the probability-ratio guarantee: with probability at least $1-\delta$ the output behaves as under pure $\varepsilon$-DP, and with probability at most $\delta$ the guarantee may fail outright (made precise by the privacy-loss tail lemma in the "Overlap Is Privacy" entry). A common rule keeps $\delta \ll 1/n$, $n$ the dataset size.

Why $\delta \ll 1/n$: Dwork & Roth warn that values of $\delta$ on the order of $1/n$ are "very dangerous: they permit 'preserving privacy' by publishing the complete records of a small number of database participants." Concretely, the mechanism "pick one of the $n$ records uniformly and publish it in full" violates any reasonable privacy notion, yet satisfies $(\varepsilon, \delta)$-DP with $\delta \approx 1/n$ — each fixed person's record appears with probability only $1/n$. So $\delta$ must be negligible compared to $1/n$ for the guarantee to mean anything per person. (source: Dwork & Roth 2014, §2.3; example: course notes)

  • Dwork & Roth, "The Algorithmic Foundations of Differential Privacy" (2014) — see the discussion of $\delta$ following Definition 2.4.

Reading $\varepsilon$

Calibration: $\varepsilon\to 0$ means identical outputs and no utility; $\varepsilon\approx 1$ is strong, practical privacy; $\varepsilon\gg 10$ is just a number. At $\varepsilon=1$, $e^{1}\approx 2.7$, so the odds shift by at most 2.7 times.

Key takeaway: $\varepsilon\approx 1$ is the practical sweet spot.

$\varepsilon$ in the Wild: Two Setups

Before comparing numbers, compare setups, because the same symbol means different things. Apple runs the local model: every iPhone randomizes its own report before it leaves the device, the data are emoji, newly typed words, and Safari domains, and the budget is spent per donation and renewed every day. The Census Bureau runs the central model: it holds every exact record for 331 million people, adds noise once to the redistricting tables, and spends one global budget for one release. Apple's 4 to 8 is per donation per day; the Census 17 is for the whole country, once.

Key takeaway: Local per-donation budgets and one central global budget are not the same kind of number.

Background & further reading

Apple (local model). Apple's whitepaper documents per-feature, per-donation budgets: $\varepsilon = 4$ for Lookup Hints (two donations/day), $\varepsilon = 4$ for emoji (one/day), $\varepsilon = 8$ for QuickType new words (two/day), $\varepsilon = 2$ for Health types (one/day), renewed daily. The mechanism is a count-mean sketch: the device hashes the item into a vector, flips each bit with probability $1/(1+e^{\varepsilon/2})$, and uploads one randomized row per donation; the server averages rows into a histogram and debiases it exactly as the coin protocol does. Reports lose their IP address on receipt, carry no device identifier, are kept for at most three months, and the system is opt-in. Only categorical items from a fixed dictionary can be learned; nothing about an individual user is estimable from one row. What came out, per Apple's 2017 report: the most-used emoji by keyboard locale (the "face with tears of joy" leading in English, the red heart in French), newly popular words not in the QuickType dictionary, and Safari domains that drain energy or auto-play video.

What Apple's number bounds. One donation: the server's likelihood ratio between "this user typed item A" and "typed item B" is at most $e^{4}\approx 55$ or $e^{8}\approx 3000$ for that one row, from that one day. It does not bound what accumulates over weeks of daily donations, which is the gap the next slide's audit exploits.

US Census 2020 (central model). The Bureau's motivation was its own reconstruction experiment on the 2010 tables: solving the published block-level counts recovered a record (block, sex, age, race, ethnicity) for every one of 308.7 million people, and matching those records to commercial data re-identified 46% of the population exactly (17% of the country, once confirmed against the confidential file). The TopDown algorithm in the Disclosure Avoidance System (DAS) then works on the full enumeration: it adds discrete Gaussian noise to the histogram of counts at every level of the geographic hierarchy (nation, state, county, tract, block group, block), then post-processes so the noisy tables are non-negative integers, consistent from block up to nation, and match a few invariants published exactly (state resident totals, the number of housing units per block, group-quarters facility counts). Accounting is in zero-concentrated DP: production $\rho = 2.63$ for the persons file, converted to $\varepsilon = 17.14$ at $\delta = 10^{-10}$, plus $\rho = 0.07$ ($\varepsilon = 2.47$) for housing units, giving the global $\varepsilon = 19.61$ on the June 9, 2021 announcement; the redistricting (P.L. 94-171) data were released August 12, 2021 for a resident population of 331,449,281. The budget is spent once: every table derived from the noisy histogram is free by post-processing.

What the Census number bounds. One person's entire record, across every published table, with the whole country as the population: the attacker's odds about any one person's row can move by at most $e^{17.14}$, an astronomically loose worst case. The Bureau's argument for the value is empirical, not the $e^{\varepsilon}$ bound: the reconstruction-plus-reidentification attack that succeeded on 2010 data falls to near the baseline guess rate on the 2020 noisy tables, while the mean absolute error of total population is a few persons for counties and places and grows only at the block level.

What $\varepsilon$ Does NOT Mean

Common misreadings: $\varepsilon$ is not the probability you are identified, it is not a percentage, and a large $\varepsilon$ is not private. It bounds a ratio of probabilities, nothing else.

Key takeaway: $\varepsilon$ is a probability-ratio bound, not a risk percentage.

Superpower 1: Post-Processing

The first superpower: anything you compute from a DP output is still DP. Round it, plot it, feed it to a model, the guarantee carries through. You cannot undo privacy by clever analysis.

Key takeaway: Post-processing a DP output can never break its privacy.

Theorem & proof

Post-processing invariance (Dwork & Roth 2014, Prop. 2.1). Let $M$ be $\varepsilon$-DP and let $f$ be any function of $M$'s output — possibly randomized, with randomness independent of $M$'s and of the data. Then $f \circ M$ is $\varepsilon$-DP.

Proof. First take $f$ deterministic. For any event $O$ in $f$'s output space and neighbors $D \sim D'$, $$\Pr[f(M(D)) \in O] \;=\; \Pr[M(D) \in f^{-1}(O)] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[M(D') \in f^{-1}(O)] \;=\; e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[f(M(D')) \in O],$$ using the DP guarantee on the event $f^{-1}(O)$. For randomized $f$, write $f(\cdot) = g(\cdot, W)$ with $W$ the independent seed; conditioning on $W = w$ makes $g(\cdot, w)$ deterministic, so the bound holds for each $w$, and averaging over $W$ preserves the inequality. $\blacksquare$ (proof: privacy course 01-dp, dp3 deck)

The same argument gives the $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ version: $f \circ M$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP whenever $M$ is.

Boundary of the superpower: post-processing means computing on the released output only. Any computation that touches the raw data $D$ again is a new query, and its cost is governed by composition (see "Many Samples Break It"), not post-processing.

What "Look Alike" Really Means

The output is not one number but a distribution. With Alice you get one bell of likely outputs; without her, a slightly shifted bell. DP forces those two bells to overlap.

Key takeaway: "Indistinguishable" means you cannot tell which bell a sample came from.

A Warm-Up: Guess the Nationality

An intuition pump. Male heights are roughly $\mathcal{N}(170,10^2)$ in Korea and $\mathcal{N}(168,10^2)$ in Japan. You meet one 171 cm person; which country? The means differ by 2 cm while each spread is 10 cm, so the bells are nearly equally tall there and your best guess barely beats a coin flip.

Key takeaway: Two close, wide distributions are hard to tell apart from one sample.

Theorem & proof

How much better than a coin flip? With equal priors on Korea ($\mathcal N(170, 10^2)$) and Japan ($\mathcal N(168, 10^2)$), the Bayes-optimal test is the likelihood-ratio test, which for equal variances thresholds at the midpoint: guess Korea iff $x \ge 169$. Its error probability is $$\Pr[X < 169 \mid \mathcal N(170,10^2)] \;=\; \Phi\!\left(\tfrac{169-170}{10}\right) \;=\; \Phi(-0.1) \;\approx\; 0.460,$$ and by symmetry the same for the other class. So the best possible accuracy from one sample is $\Phi(0.1) \approx 54\%$ — barely above the 50% coin flip, exactly as the slide claims. (derivation: course notes)

This is the quantitative template for DP: privacy holds when the "with Alice" and "without Alice" output distributions are two close, wide bells, so that even the optimal distinguisher is near chance.

Overlap Is Privacy

The more two distributions overlap, the less any test can separate them. In DP, output-with-Alice and output-without-Alice are two bells, and DP caps the gap at $e^{\varepsilon}$ in the odds.

Key takeaway: Small $\varepsilon$ forces overlap, and Alice hides in it.

Definitions

Privacy loss random variable (source: privacy course dp4). For neighbors $D \sim D'$, define the privacy loss function at output $y$: $$I_{D,D'}(y) \;=\; \log \frac{\Pr[M(D) = y]}{\Pr[M(D') = y]}$$ (densities in the continuous case). The privacy loss random variable is $I_{D,D'}(Y)$ with $Y \sim M(D)$ — the log-likelihood ratio the best possible adversary observes. Pure $\varepsilon$-DP is exactly the statement $|I_{D,D'}(y)| \le \varepsilon$ for all $y$ and all neighboring pairs.

Theorem & proof

Tail lemma (source: privacy course dp4): if for all neighbors $\Pr[I_{D,D'}(Y) > \varepsilon] \le \delta$, then $M$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP.

Proof. Fix neighbors $D, D'$ and any event $S$. Split outputs into the good set $G = \{y : I_{D,D'}(y) \le \varepsilon\}$ and the bad set $B = G^c$, which has $\Pr[M(D) \in B] \le \delta$ by assumption. Then $$\Pr[M(D) \in S] \;\le\; \Pr[M(D) \in S \cap G] + \Pr[M(D) \in B] \;\le\; \Pr[M(D) \in S \cap G] + \delta.$$ On $G$ the pointwise ratio is at most $e^{\varepsilon}$, so $\Pr[M(D) \in S \cap G] \le e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[M(D') \in S \cap G] \le e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[M(D') \in S]$. Combining, $\Pr[M(D) \in S] \le e^{\varepsilon} \Pr[M(D') \in S] + \delta$. $\blacksquare$ (proof: privacy course 01-dp, dp4 deck)

This is the precise sense in which "overlap is privacy": DP caps the adversary's log-odds at $\varepsilon$ except on a $\delta$-probability tail, and it is also the engine of the Gaussian mechanism's proof later.

Theorem & proof: repeated queries

Why 1000 samples separate the bells: the sample mean of $n = 1000$ heights has standard deviation $10/\sqrt{1000} \approx 0.32$ cm, far below the 2 cm gap — the bells of the averages barely overlap, so the test becomes near-perfect. (derivation: course notes)

Sequential (basic) composition (Dwork, McSherry, Nissim, Smith, TCC 2006; adaptive form). Let $M_1$ be $\varepsilon_1$-DP, and let $M_2(\cdot, a_1)$ be $\varepsilon_2$-DP for every fixed value $a_1$ of $M_1$'s output (so the second query may depend on the first answer). Then the joint release $M(D) = (M_1(D),\, M_2(D, M_1(D)))$ is $(\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2)$-DP.

Proof. For discrete outputs and neighbors $D \sim D'$, factorize by the chain rule: $$\Pr[M(D) = (a_1, a_2)] \;=\; \Pr[M_1(D) = a_1]\; \Pr[M_2(D, a_1) = a_2].$$ The first factor is at most $e^{\varepsilon_1} \Pr[M_1(D') = a_1]$ by $M_1$'s guarantee; the second is at most $e^{\varepsilon_2} \Pr[M_2(D', a_1) = a_2]$ by $M_2$'s guarantee at the fixed value $a_1$. Hence $$\Pr[M(D) = (a_1, a_2)] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon_1 + \varepsilon_2}\, \Pr[M(D') = (a_1, a_2)],$$ and summing this pointwise bound over any set $T$ of output pairs gives the DP inequality for $T$. $\blacksquare$ (proof: privacy course 01-dp, dp3 deck)

Corollary ($k$-fold). $k$ adaptively chosen releases, each $\varepsilon_i$-DP, jointly satisfy $\left(\sum_{i=1}^k \varepsilon_i\right)$-DP; with $(\varepsilon_i, \delta_i)$ guarantees, the sums $\left(\sum_i \varepsilon_i, \sum_i \delta_i\right)$ compose likewise. This is why $\varepsilon$ is called a budget: every query spends some, and the spend adds up.

What DP Gives and Doesn't

DP gives protection against any side knowledge, survival under post-processing, and one tunable knob. It does not hide population facts, make every release useful, or help when $\varepsilon$ is huge.

Key takeaway: Know both the guarantees and the limits of DP.

Individuals, Not the Crowd

DP hides whether you are in the data, not what the population looks like. "Smokers get cancer" stays learnable; "Alice smokes" does not.

Key takeaway: DP protects individuals while letting us learn population trends.

04 — Achieving DP: Add Randomness

Fourth section. How do we actually build DP algorithms? The answer is calibrated noise, called mechanisms.

Key takeaway: DP is achieved by adding carefully calibrated randomness.

The Recipe

Every DP mechanism follows one shape: compute the answer, add randomness, release. The crucial word is "calibrated," the noise is not arbitrary.

Key takeaway: Compute, add calibrated noise, release.

Randomized Response

Randomized response is privacy from before computers, a 1965 survey technique by Warner for sensitive yes/no questions. It is still the cleanest intuition for DP.

Key takeaway: Randomized response is DP's oldest and clearest example.

Definitions

Randomized response, general form (source: privacy course dp2). Each respondent holds a sensitive bit $D_i \in \{0,1\}$ and reports $$Y_i \;=\; \begin{cases} D_i & \text{w.p. } \tfrac12 + \gamma \\[2pt] 1 - D_i & \text{w.p. } \tfrac12 - \gamma \end{cases} \qquad 0 < \gamma \le \tfrac12,$$ with each flip independent. The dial $\gamma$ trades privacy for utility: $\gamma = \tfrac12$ means $Y_i = D_i$ always (full utility, zero privacy); $\gamma = 0$ means $Y_i$ is a fair coin (perfect privacy, zero utility).

Theorem & proof

Theorem. Randomized response $M(D) = (Y_1, \dots, Y_n)$ is $\varepsilon_\gamma$-DP with $$\varepsilon_\gamma \;=\; \ln\frac{\tfrac12+\gamma}{\tfrac12-\gamma},$$ and for small $\gamma$, $\varepsilon_\gamma = 4\gamma + O(\gamma^3)$ — the "$4\gamma$-DP" reading. (The proof is given under "The Coin Protocol" below for $\gamma = \tfrac14$; the general case is identical with $\gamma$ in place of $\tfrac14$. Source: privacy course 01-dp, dp2 deck.)

Background & further reading

  • Warner, "Randomized Response: A Survey Technique for Eliminating Evasive Answer Bias" (JASA Vol. 60, No. 309, March 1965, pp. 63–69) — the original paper, four decades before DP; Warner's goal was honest survey answers to sensitive questions, and the privacy guarantee came as a by-product.

The Coin Protocol

Read the tree left to right. The respondent holds a secret yes-or-no answer and flips a fair coin in private. Heads, probability one half: they answer truthfully. Tails: they flip a second coin and let it answer for them, heads says "yes", tails says "no", each with probability one quarter overall. So the report equals the truth with probability one half plus one quarter, three quarters. The surveyor sees only the final word and none of the flips, so every "yes" might be coin 2 talking.

Key takeaway: Truth three times in four, and the surveyor cannot tell which three.

Definitions

The coin protocol is the $\gamma = \tfrac14$ instance of randomized response: on heads (probability $\tfrac12$) answer truthfully; on tails, answer by a second fair coin. So the truth is reported w.p. $\tfrac12 + \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac34 = \tfrac12 + \gamma$ with $\gamma = \tfrac14$. Generalizing, if coin 1 lands heads w.p. $q$, the truth is reported w.p. $(1+q)/2$ and $\varepsilon = \ln\frac{1+q}{1-q}$, i.e. $q = \tanh(\varepsilon/2)$; this is the parametrization used by the course's coin simulator.

Theorem & proof

Theorem. The coin protocol is $\varepsilon$-DP with $\varepsilon = \ln 3 \approx 1.10$.

Proof. Neighbors differ in one respondent's bit, say respondent $i$'s: $D_i = 1$ vs $D_i' = 0$. Reports are independent, and only factor $i$ differs between the two worlds, so every other factor cancels in the ratio: $$\frac{\Pr[M(D) = y]}{\Pr[M(D') = y]} \;=\; \frac{\Pr[Y_i = y_i \mid D_i = 1]}{\Pr[Y_i = y_i \mid D_i' = 0]} \;\in\; \left\{ \frac{3/4}{1/4},\; \frac{1/4}{3/4} \right\},$$ since respondent $i$ reports a given value w.p. $\tfrac34$ (it matches their bit) or $\tfrac14$ (it is the flip). Both ratios lie in $[e^{-\ln 3}, e^{\ln 3}]$; summing the pointwise bound over any event $O$ gives $\Pr[M(D) \in O] \le 3\Pr[M(D') \in O]$, i.e. $(\ln 3)$-DP — and equality holds when $y_i = D_i$, so $\ln 3$ is exact. The general-$\gamma$ proof replaces $\tfrac34/\tfrac14$ by $(\tfrac12+\gamma)/(\tfrac12-\gamma)$, giving $\varepsilon_\gamma$ from the previous entry. $\blacksquare$ (proof: privacy course 01-dp, dp2 deck)

Recovering the Truth

Half answer truthfully and half flip a fair coin, forcing 25% "yes." Solving $0.70=\tfrac12 p+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12$ gives $p=0.90$. So observing 70% "yes" implies a true rate of 90%.

Key takeaway: Known noise can be subtracted to recover the true aggregate.

Theorem & proof

Debiasing. Each report has $\mathbb{E}[Y_i] = D_i(\tfrac12+\gamma) + (1-D_i)(\tfrac12-\gamma) = 2\gamma D_i + \tfrac12 - \gamma$ — affine in $D_i$ with slope $2\gamma$, hence invertible for any $\gamma > 0$. Inverting gives the unbiased per-person estimator $$\hat D_i \;=\; \frac{Y_i - \tfrac12 + \gamma}{2\gamma}, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\hat D_i] = D_i,$$ and $\hat p = \tfrac1n\sum_i \hat D_i$ satisfies $\mathbb{E}[\hat p] = p$, the true fraction. In the coin protocol ($\gamma = \tfrac14$): $\mathbb{E}[\bar Y] = \tfrac{p}{2} + \tfrac14$, so $\hat p = 2\bar Y - \tfrac12$ — with $\bar Y = 0.70$ this is $0.90$, exactly the slide's arithmetic.

Accuracy. Since $\mathrm{Var}[Y_i] \le \tfrac14$ (a Bernoulli variable), $$\mathrm{Var}[\hat p] \;=\; \frac{1}{4n^2\gamma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathrm{Var}[Y_i] \;\le\; \frac{1}{16\,n\gamma^2}, \qquad \text{std.\ dev.} \le \frac{1}{4\gamma\sqrt n}.$$ Chebyshev's inequality then gives $\Pr[|\hat p - p| > \alpha] \le \beta$ once $n \ge \dfrac{1}{16\,\gamma^2\beta\,\alpha^2}$. Anchor numbers: $\gamma = 0.1$, $\alpha = \beta = 0.05$ requires $n = 50{,}000$ respondents — privacy is paid for in sample size, at rate $n \propto 1/\gamma^2$. (proof: privacy course 01-dp, dp2 deck)

Why It's Private

Any single "yes" might just be a coin flip, so the individual is protected while the aggregate rate survives. Noise hides people, not the population.

Key takeaway: Privacy and utility coexist, at the cost of some noise.

Definitions: local vs central DP

Local differential privacy. A local randomizer $R$ is $\varepsilon$-LDP if for all pairs of individual inputs $x, x'$ and all output events $S$: $$\Pr[R(x) \in S] \;\le\; e^{\varepsilon}\, \Pr[R(x') \in S].$$ Each person randomizes their own record before it leaves their device; the curator only ever sees noisy reports, so no trusted party is needed. Randomized response is the canonical $\varepsilon_\gamma$-LDP mechanism. Central DP instead lets a trusted curator see raw data and add a single dose of noise to the aggregate.

Theorem & proof

The price of distrust (source: privacy course dp2, central-vs-local comparison). Estimating a fraction $p$ from $n$ bits:

  • Local (RR): one coin per user; per-user noise variances add, giving typical error $\approx \dfrac{1}{4\gamma\sqrt n}$.
  • Central (Laplace): one $\mathrm{Lap}(0, \tfrac{1}{n\varepsilon})$ draw on the final average (the mean has sensitivity $1/n$), giving typical error $\approx \dfrac{1}{n\varepsilon}$.

Trusting a curator buys a full $\sqrt n$ factor of accuracy — a gap fundamental to the local model, not an artifact of randomized response.

The Laplace Mechanism

To release a number privately, add noise around the true value: $\tilde q = q + \text{noise}$, with noise proportional to $\Delta q/\varepsilon$. Smaller $\varepsilon$ or bigger sensitivity means more noise.

Key takeaway: Noise scales with sensitivity over $\varepsilon$.

Definitions

Laplace distribution. $\mathrm{Lap}(0, b)$ has density $\frac{1}{2b}e^{-|z|/b}$ on $\mathbb{R}$, mean $0$, variance $2b^2$, and tail $\Pr[|Z| > t\,b] = e^{-t}$.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (Dwork, McSherry, Nissim & Smith, TCC 2006). For $f : \mathcal X^n \to \mathbb{R}^k$ with $\ell_1$ sensitivity $\Delta_1$, the mechanism $$M(D) \;=\; f(D) + (Y_1, \dots, Y_k), \qquad Y_i \overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} \mathrm{Lap}(0,\, \Delta_1/\varepsilon)$$ is $\varepsilon$-DP.

Proof. With $b = \Delta_1/\varepsilon$, independence gives the product density $f_{M(D)}(z) = \prod_{i=1}^k \frac{1}{2b}\exp(-|z_i - f(D)_i|/b)$. For neighbors $D \sim D'$ and any $z \in \mathbb{R}^k$, $$\frac{f_{M(D)}(z)}{f_{M(D')}(z)} \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^k \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{|z_i - f(D')_i| - |z_i - f(D)_i|}{b}\Bigr) \;\le\; \prod_{i=1}^k \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{|f(D)_i - f(D')_i|}{b}\Bigr) \;=\; \exp\!\Bigl(\frac{\|f(D)-f(D')\|_1}{b}\Bigr),$$ where the inequality is the triangle inequality $|z - a'| - |z - a| \le |a - a'|$ applied per coordinate — the observation $z$ drops out entirely. By the definition of sensitivity, $\|f(D)-f(D')\|_1 \le \Delta_1$, and the calibration $b = \Delta_1/\varepsilon$ makes the bound $e^{\Delta_1/b} = e^{\varepsilon}$. Integrating the pointwise density bound over any event $T \subseteq \mathbb{R}^k$ gives $\Pr[M(D) \in T] \le e^{\varepsilon}\Pr[M(D') \in T]$. $\blacksquare$ (proof: privacy course 01-dp, dp2 deck)

Accuracy. Per coordinate the noise has variance $2\Delta_1^2/\varepsilon^2$, and the Laplace tail gives $$\Pr\!\Bigl[\,|M(D)_i - f(D)_i| > \frac{\Delta_1}{\varepsilon}\ln\frac{1}{\beta}\Bigr] \;=\; \beta,$$ so with probability $1-\beta$ the error is at most $(\Delta_1/\varepsilon)\ln(1/\beta)$ — independent of the dataset size $n$. (source: privacy course dp2)

Background & further reading

  • Dwork, McSherry, Nissim & Smith, "Calibrating Noise to Sensitivity in Private Data Analysis" (TCC 2006) — the paper that introduced both the Laplace mechanism and differential privacy itself.

Worked example: a noisy count

Concrete calibration. A count has $\Delta_1 = 1$, so at $\varepsilon = 1$ the Laplace mechanism adds $\mathrm{Lap}(0, 1)$ noise. By the Laplace tail bound above with $\beta = 0.01$: with probability $99\%$ the reported count is within $\ln 100 \approx 4.6$ of the truth — whether the hospital has $1{,}000$ patients or $10$ million. The absolute error is constant in $n$, so the relative error vanishes as data grows. (source: privacy course dp2)

Meanwhile any one patient shifts the true count by exactly $1$, well inside the typical noise magnitude — that is the precise sense in which "1003" hides whether you are in the 1000.

Sensitivity

Sensitivity $\Delta q$ is how much one person can change the answer. "How many patients?" changes by 1, so a count has sensitivity 1; an average or sum can be much higher. Calibrate noise to the worst-case one-person change.

Key takeaway: Sensitivity is the worst-case effect of a single person.

Definitions

$\ell_1$ sensitivity. For $f : \mathcal X^n \to \mathbb{R}^k$, $$\Delta_1 \;=\; \max_{D,\,D' \text{ neighbors}} \|f(D) - f(D')\|_1.$$ $\ell_2$ sensitivity. $\Delta_2 = \max_{D \sim D'} \|f(D) - f(D')\|_2$. Always $\Delta_2 \le \Delta_1 \le \sqrt{k}\,\Delta_2$; the Gaussian mechanism pays $\Delta_2$, Laplace pays $\Delta_1$, and in high dimension the $\sqrt{k}$ gap is why Gaussian noise wins in ML. Both are properties of the query $f$ alone, computable before seeing any data. (source: privacy course dp2/dp4)

Theorem & proof

Worked examples (source: privacy course dp2):

  • Counting query $f(D) = |\{i : X_i \text{ has the property}\}|$: adding, removing, or changing one record moves the count by at most $1$, so $\Delta_1 = 1$ at any dataset size.
  • Mean query $f(D) = \tfrac1n \sum_i X_i$ with $X_i \in [a,b]$ (replace convention, fixed $n$): $|f(D) - f(D')| = |X_i' - X_i|/n \le (b-a)/n$ — the mean's sensitivity shrinks like $1/n$, which is why averages get cheap to privatize on large datasets.

Gaussian Mechanism

The other standard choice is bell-curve noise. The Gaussian mechanism pairs naturally with $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP and is the workhorse inside private deep learning. Same recipe, different noise shape.

Key takeaway: Gaussian noise is the default for private deep learning.

Definitions

Gaussian mechanism. $M(D) = f(D) + \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2 I)$ for $f : \mathcal X^n \to \mathbb{R}^d$, calibrated to the $\ell_2$ sensitivity $\Delta_2$. Gaussian noise cannot give pure $\varepsilon$-DP — the likelihood ratio of two shifted Gaussians diverges in the tail — which is exactly what the $\delta$ in $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP absorbs. (source: privacy course dp4)

Theorem & proof

Theorem (Dwork & Roth 2014, Thm. 3.22). For $\varepsilon \in (0,1)$ and $$\sigma \;\ge\; \frac{\Delta_2\sqrt{2\ln(1.25/\delta)}}{\varepsilon},$$ the Gaussian mechanism is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP.

Proof sketch (structure from privacy course dp4, which carries the full proof). (1) Reduce to one dimension: by rotation invariance of $\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2 I)$, only the component along the shift direction $f(D)-f(D')$ matters; let $K = \|f(D)-f(D')\|_2 \le \Delta_2$. (2) Compute the privacy loss $I(y)$: subtracting two Gaussian log-densities leaves a function linear in $y$, so $I(Y)$ is itself Gaussian — with mean $K^2/2\sigma^2$ and variance $K^2/\sigma^2$. (3) Apply the Gaussian tail bound to $\Pr[I(Y) > \varepsilon]$; the stated $\sigma$ (the $1.25 = 5/4$ gives the needed slack) makes this tail at most $\delta$ whenever $\varepsilon < 1$. (4) Invoke the tail lemma from "Overlap Is Privacy" to convert the tail bound into $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP. $\square$

The complete calculation is Theorem A.1 in Appendix A of Dwork & Roth, The Algorithmic Foundations of Differential Privacy (free PDF). Note the $\varepsilon < 1$ restriction is real: for $\varepsilon \ge 1$ this calibration is not valid, and modern treatments use Rényi-DP or optimal ("analytic") Gaussian-mechanism calibrations instead.

Why it is the deep-learning default: gradients live in very high dimension $d$, where $\Delta_2 \le \Delta_1 \le \sqrt d\,\Delta_2$ makes $\ell_2$ calibration up to $\sqrt d$ quieter than Laplace — and Gaussian noise composes tightly across thousands of steps under the accountants of Section 05.

05 — Private Machine Learning

Fifth section. Now we apply all of this to training models under a privacy budget.

Key takeaway: The same DP ideas extend from statistics to model training.

From Statistics to Models

A trained model is just a very complex query over the data, millions of numbers computed from records. It carries the same leakage risk as any statistic, so the same DP idea applies.

Key takeaway: A model is one big query, so DP can protect it.

The Naive Hope Fails

The naive hope is "the model generalizes, so it won't memorize." That is false: recall GPT-2, the canary, the diffusion portraits. Generalization and memorization happen together.

Key takeaway: Generalizing does not prevent memorizing.

DP-SGD

DP-SGD, from Abadi and colleagues in 2016, is the workhorse. Compute a per-example gradient, clip it to a maximum length, add Gaussian noise to the batch sum, take the step, and track the spent $\varepsilon$.

Key takeaway: DP-SGD = clip, add noise, step, account.

Definitions

DP-SGD (single-sample form analyzed in the privacy course, dp5). Inputs: dataset $D$ of $n$ records, loss $\ell$, step size $\alpha$, steps $T$, clip norm $G$, noise scale $\sigma$. Initialize $\theta_0$; for $t = 1, \dots, T$:

  • Sample $x_t \sim D$ uniformly; compute the per-sample gradient $g_t = \nabla_\theta \ell(\theta_{t-1}; x_t)$.
  • Clip: $\bar g_t = g_t \cdot \min(1,\, G/\|g_t\|_2)$.
  • Noise: $\tilde g_t = \bar g_t + Z$, $Z \sim \mathcal N(0, \sigma^2 I)$.
  • Update $\theta_t = \theta_{t-1} - \alpha\,\tilde g_t$ (with projection if constrained).

Abadi et al.'s production version replaces the single sample by a random "lot" (minibatch) and adds noise to the clipped gradient sum; the analysis is the same three ingredients with lot-sampling rate $q$. The noise multiplier is $\sigma/G$, the knob reported in practice.

Theorem & proof

Theorem (DP-SGD privacy; privacy course dp5). DP-SGD above is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP if $$\sigma \;\ge\; \frac{2G\sqrt{2T\log(1/\delta)}}{n\varepsilon}.$$

Proof sketch (three-step accounting, labeled a sketch; source: privacy course 01-dp, dp5 deck):

  • Step 1 — one step is a Gaussian mechanism. If the differing record is the one sampled, both clipped gradients have norm at most $G$, so $\|\bar g_t - \bar g_t'\|_2 \le 2G = \Delta_2$; the Gaussian-mechanism theorem gives a per-step cost $\varepsilon_0 \approx 2G\sqrt{2\log(1/\delta)}/\sigma$.
  • Step 2 — subsampling amplifies. The differing record is touched only with probability $q = 1/n$ per step; running an $(\varepsilon_0,\delta_0)$-DP mechanism on a random $q$-fraction costs roughly $(q\varepsilon_0,\, q\delta_0) = (\varepsilon_0/n,\, \delta_0/n)$.
  • Step 3 — compose $T$ adaptive steps. $\sqrt T$-style (advanced) composition gives total $\approx (\sqrt T\,\varepsilon_0/n,\; T\delta_0/n)$; solving $\varepsilon \approx \sqrt T\,\varepsilon_0/n$ for $\sigma$ yields the stated bound. $\square$

Abadi et al.'s moments accountant replaces step 3's generic composition with a tight computation of the privacy-loss moment generating function, saving large constant factors — it is what made $\varepsilon$ single-digit for real deep models.

Background & further reading

  • Abadi, Chu, Goodfellow, McMahan, Mironov, Talwar & Zhang, "Deep Learning with Differential Privacy" (CCS 2016) — the DP-SGD paper: clipping, lot subsampling, and the moments accountant.

Theorem & proof: the privacy accountant

Basic composition (proved under "Overlap Is Privacy"): $k$ adaptive releases with budgets $(\varepsilon_i, \delta_i)$ jointly satisfy $\big(\sum_i \varepsilon_i,\, \sum_i \delta_i\big)$-DP — linear growth.

Advanced composition (Dwork & Roth 2014, Thm. 3.20). For all $\varepsilon, \delta, \delta' \ge 0$, $k$-fold adaptive composition of $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP mechanisms satisfies $(\varepsilon', k\delta + \delta')$-DP with $$\varepsilon' \;=\; \sqrt{2k\ln(1/\delta')}\;\varepsilon \;+\; k\varepsilon(e^{\varepsilon}-1).$$ For small $\varepsilon$ the first term dominates: the budget grows like $\sqrt k\,\varepsilon$ rather than $k\varepsilon$, by allowing another tiny failure probability $\delta'$. This $\sqrt T$ growth is what step 3 of the DP-SGD accounting uses.

Further reading: accounting

  • Dwork & Roth, The Algorithmic Foundations of Differential Privacy, Thm. 3.20 (free PDF) — advanced composition, with proof.
  • Abadi et al. (CCS 2016) — the moments accountant: track the log-MGF ("moments") of the privacy-loss random variable per step, add them across steps, and convert to $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ once at the end — much tighter than applying advanced composition step by step.
  • Mironov, "Rényi Differential Privacy" (CSF 2017) — the clean formalization: measure privacy as a bound on Rényi divergence of order $\alpha$; RDP composes by simple addition and converts to $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP at the end. Modern DP libraries account in RDP.

Why Clip, Why Noise?

Clipping bounds one example's influence so no single record dominates the step; that bound is the sensitivity. Noise then hides each example's contribution, giving every gradient step the same two-worlds deniability.

Key takeaway: Clip to cap each example's effect; noise to hide it.

Definitions

Clipping. $\bar g = g \cdot \min\!\big(1,\, G/\|g\|_2\big)$: the direction is preserved, the norm is capped at $G$.

Theorem & proof

Why clipping is necessary. Calibrated noise needs the gradient's sensitivity, but deep networks admit no useful a-priori Lipschitz constant: $\sup_x \|\nabla_\theta \ell(\theta; x)\|_2$ is effectively unbounded, and one outlier could dominate an update. Clipping does not assume a bound — it enforces one: for neighbors, if the differing record is sampled, the triangle inequality gives $$\|\bar g_t - \bar g_t'\|_2 \;\le\; \|\bar g_t\|_2 + \|\bar g_t'\|_2 \;\le\; 2G,$$ which is exactly the finite $\ell_2$ sensitivity the Gaussian mechanism needs. (source: privacy course dp5)

The cost. Whenever $\|g\|_2 > G$ the clipped gradient is a biased estimate of the true gradient — DP-SGD deliberately trades unbiasedness for bounded sensitivity ("privacy first, unbiasedness second"). The noise then hides which record was present, giving every gradient step the two-worlds guarantee.

The Utility Cost

DP-SGD models are less accurate, and smaller $\varepsilon$ means a bigger gap; that gap is precisely the cost of privacy. Early DP-SGD paid a lot on benchmarks like CIFAR-10, but better methods plus pretraining keep shrinking it year by year.

Key takeaway: The private-vs-non-private accuracy gap is the price of privacy, and it is falling.

More Data Helps

Privacy gets cheaper as the dataset grows. The noise is per-step, not per-person, so with more people each one matters less and the per-person noise washes out.

Key takeaway: Big datasets make DP cheaper.

Theorem & proof

Read it directly off the DP-SGD noise bound $\sigma \ge \dfrac{2G\sqrt{2T\log(1/\delta)}}{n\varepsilon}$: at a fixed privacy target $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ and step count $T$, the required noise scales like $\sigma \propto 1/n$ — ten times the data, one tenth the noise. Equivalently, at fixed noise $\sigma$ the achieved budget is $\varepsilon \approx \dfrac{2G\sqrt{2T\log(1/\delta)}}{n\sigma} \propto 1/n$. (derivation: course notes, direct rearrangement of the dp5 theorem)

The mechanism is subsampling amplification: with more people, the chance any given step touches your record is $1/n$, so each person's exposure per step shrinks with the population. This is the deep reason DP is most practical exactly where the stakes are largest — population-scale datasets.

06 — Frontier 2025–26

Final section. The current research frontier: can foundation models themselves be made private?

Key takeaway: Privacy for foundation models is the open frontier.

Private Fine-Tuning Works

Good news first: DP fine-tuning of large pretrained models reaches near non-private accuracy. Pretrain on public data, fine-tune privately, with steady momentum through 2023-25.

Key takeaway: Pretrain publicly, fine-tune privately, lose little accuracy.

Definitions

The public-pretrain / private-fine-tune split. The DP guarantee covers only the fine-tuning dataset: neighbors differ in one fine-tuning record, while the pretraining corpus is treated as public and gets no protection. This is what makes the accuracy numbers good — most of the model's capability is bought with unprotected data — and it is also the caveat to keep in mind when the "public" corpus is scraped web text (see "The Web-Scale Puzzle").

Background & further reading

  • Yu et al., "Differentially Private Fine-tuning of Language Models" (ICLR 2022) — parameter-efficient DP fine-tuning (e.g. LoRA-style adapters) of large pretrained LMs approaching non-private utility at single-digit $\varepsilon$.
  • Li, Tramèr, Liang & Hashimoto, "Large Language Models Can Be Strong Differentially Private Learners" — full DP fine-tuning made to work via careful hyperparameters and memory-efficient ("ghost") clipping; bigger pretrained models turn out to be easier, not harder, to fine-tune privately.

Private Pretraining Arrives

In 2025 Google released VaultGemma, a 1B-parameter LLM pretrained from scratch with DP-SGD under a sequence-level guarantee of $\varepsilon \le 2$, guided by new scaling laws for DP training. Its utility sits near non-private models from about five years earlier — a real gap, but now a measured one.

Key takeaway: Fully DP pretraining is now demonstrated, at a quantified utility cost.

Definitions

Unit of privacy. VaultGemma's guarantee is sequence-level $(\varepsilon \le 2.0,\ \delta \le 1.1\times 10^{-10})$-DP, where a sequence is 1024 consecutive tokens from the training stream. Sequence-level is weaker than user-level: information a person spreads across many sequences (or many documents) is not covered by the single-sequence guarantee — the same unit-of-privacy question the web-scale entry raises.

Background & further reading

  • Google Research blog, "VaultGemma" (Sept. 12, 2025) — the announcement: a 1B-parameter open model, described as the largest model trained from scratch with differential privacy, with the guarantee above and new DP scaling laws used to choose the training configuration. The utility comparison to roughly five-year-old non-private models is the blog's own framing.

Private Synthetic Data

Another approach: generate a safe, shareable dataset under DP. The generator carries the privacy guarantee, you train freely on its output, and post-processing keeps it private. This is deployed: Apple uses DP synthetic data to improve Apple Intelligence text features (2025).

Key takeaway: DP synthetic data lets you share sensitive datasets safely.

Theorem & proof

Why "train freely on its output" is rigorous. Suppose the generator-training procedure $G$ is $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP as a function of the sensitive dataset $D$. Sampling synthetic data from the released generator and training any downstream model on those samples is a (randomized) function of $G$'s output alone — it never touches $D$ again. By post-processing invariance (proved under "Superpower 1"), the downstream model, and anything built from it, inherits the same $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ guarantee with no further budget spent. (derivation: course notes, direct corollary of the post-processing theorem)

The fine print: the guarantee only covers what flowed through $G$. If the synthetic pipeline is tuned by repeatedly comparing against the real data, those comparisons are new queries and must be accounted.

Background & further reading

  • Apple, "Understanding Aggregate Trends for Apple Intelligence Using Differential Privacy" (Apr. 14, 2025) — the deployment the slide cites: DP over anonymous device signals for short Genmoji-style prompts, and DP-selected synthetic emails for longer text features, "without Apple collecting emails from the device."

Auditing Privacy

We can estimate the real $\varepsilon$ by attacking the model: run membership attacks and measure the leak. Sometimes the guarantee is loose. The motto is trust, but verify.

Key takeaway: Audit the empirical $\varepsilon$, do not just trust the proof.

Theorem & proof

From attacks to a lower bound on $\varepsilon$. The hypothesis-testing bound from "Membership Inference" says any attack against an $(\varepsilon,\delta)$-DP mechanism obeys $\mathrm{TPR} \le e^{\varepsilon}\,\mathrm{FPR} + \delta$. Run a concrete attack (e.g. membership inference against planted canaries) and measure its operating point $(\mathrm{FPR}, \mathrm{TPR})$; then any $\varepsilon$ the system could truthfully claim must satisfy $$\hat\varepsilon \;\ge\; \ln\!\frac{\mathrm{TPR} - \delta}{\mathrm{FPR}}.$$ (derivation: course notes, inverting the hypothesis-testing bound.) The audit thus produces a certified lower bound on the true privacy loss: if $\hat\varepsilon$ exceeds the claimed $\varepsilon$, the implementation is broken (a real failure mode — clipping bugs, correlated noise, wrong sampling); if it is far below, the analysis may simply be loose.

Background & further reading

  • Jagielski, Ullman & Oprea, "Auditing Differentially Private Machine Learning: How Private is Private SGD?" — the auditing methodology: data-poisoning attacks turned into quantitative lower bounds on DP-SGD's real-world $\varepsilon$.

Unlearning as Plan B

If you cannot prevent memorization, can you delete it later? Machine unlearning aims to remove one person after training without retraining from scratch.

Key takeaway: Unlearning is the fallback when prevention fails.

Definitions

Exact unlearning. An unlearning procedure $U$ is exact if for every dataset $D$ and record $x \in D$, the distribution of $U(\text{model trained on } D,\, x)$ equals the distribution of training from scratch on $D \setminus \{x\}$ — the deleted person's influence is provably gone, not just attenuated. Approximate notions relax equality to closeness (often in a DP-style $(\varepsilon,\delta)$ sense).

SISA (Sharded, Isolated, Sliced, Aggregated): partition the data into shards, train an isolated constituent model per shard (checkpointing along data "slices"), and aggregate their predictions. Deleting a record requires retraining only its shard, from the checkpoint before the record's slice — exact unlearning at a fraction of full retraining cost.

Background & further reading

  • Bourtoule et al., "Machine Unlearning" (IEEE S&P 2021) — the SISA framework and the case for exact, verifiable deletion.

Privacy in the Stack

Some requests are too big for the phone: a large language model, a long document. Where does the data go? Apple's 2024 answer is Private Cloud Compute. The device tries its on-device model first. If it must go out, the request is encrypted end to end and can only be decrypted by a server that has cryptographically proved it is running a published software image on Apple silicon with a Secure Enclave and a locked-down OS. That node answers and then forgets: nothing is stored, and there is no shell, debugger, or admin login for anyone at Apple. This is the same idea as confidential computing: trusted hardware plus remote attestation. Then the punchline: secure hardware bounds who can look at the data, DP bounds what a released output reveals. Different threats, different tools.

Key takeaway: Hardware controls who can observe the computation; DP controls what the output reveals.

Background & further reading

  • Apple Security Research blog, "Private Cloud Compute: A new frontier for AI privacy in the cloud" (June 10, 2024) — Apple's system for cloud AI inference on custom Apple-silicon servers; the device's request is encrypted to the public keys of attested PCC nodes only, and requests are routed through a third-party relay so that the node cannot see the requester's IP address.
  • Confidential computing (e.g. Intel TDX, AMD SEV-SNP, Arm CCA, NVIDIA confidential GPUs) provides the general form: a hardware-isolated trusted execution environment whose code identity is attested to the client before data is released to it.

Apple's four stated requirements (folded in here after the separate slide was dropped, 2026-09-08): stateless computation, the request data are used only to answer that request and never retained; enforceable guarantees / no privileged runtime access, no remote shells or debug paths that bypass the protections, even for Apple staff; non-targetability, an attacker, including an insider, cannot steer one particular user's requests to a compromised node; verifiable transparency, every production software image is published in a tamper-proof log so outside researchers can check that the running code is the promised code. Complementarity, precisely: confidential/trusted hardware controls who can observe the computation; DP bounds what any released output reveals about one person. PCC without DP still returns exact answers; DP without hardware protection still exposes raw data to the curator.

The Law Is Catching Up

Regulation is pushing DP and auditing into practice: GDPR's right to be forgotten, the EU AI Act — whose duties for general-purpose AI models apply since August 2025 — and a wave of US state privacy laws.

Key takeaway: Law is increasingly forcing privacy engineering into products.

Background & further reading

  • GDPR Article 17 — Right to erasure ("right to be forgotten") — the legal driver behind machine unlearning: data subjects can demand deletion of their personal data, and "we already trained on it" is not an exemption.
  • Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the EU AI Act), official text on EUR-Lex — obligations for general-purpose AI model providers (training-data documentation and copyright/privacy-relevant transparency among them) apply from August 2, 2025, as the deck states.

The regulatory logic connects back to the technical content: DP gives a provable answer to "does the model leak person X?", and auditing (two entries back) gives the empirical check — exactly the kind of evidence documentation-style obligations ask for.
